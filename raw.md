## Chapter 1: Forensic Medicine & Medical Jurisprudence

### Q: What do you know about medical jurisprudence? What is the meaning of the term and its scope?
*(Credit: Dedcatto)*

**Answer:**  
**Medical Jurisprudence:** *(juris = law; prudential = knowledge)* [1].  
Medical jurisprudence is the science that deals with the application of rules and regulations for the guidance of medical professionals [1]. It deals with the relationships which have legal consequences, e.g., doctor-doctor relationship, doctor-patient relationship, doctor-state relationship [1].  
*Or,* Medical jurisprudence deals with legal responsibilities of physician/doctor with particular reference to those arising from physician-patient relationship/doctor-patient relationship, e.g., medical negligence cases, consent, rights and duties of doctors, serious professional misconduct, medical ethics, etc. In short, medical jurisprudence deals with legal aspects of practice of medicine [1].

### Q: What is the hierarchy and jurisdiction/powers of Criminal Courts in Bangladesh?
*(Credit: Dedcatto)*

**Answer:**  
**Hierarchy and Powers of Criminal Courts in Bangladesh:**  
1. **Supreme Court:**  
   * *Appellate Division:* Can consider criminal appeal cases; all appeal cases come after being dealt by High Court Division [4, 5].  
   * *High Court Division:* Can deal any case; can award any punishment provided in BPC; death sentence must be approved by High Court passed in lower court [5].  
2. **Session and Judge Court:**  
   * *District Session and Judge Court:* Can deal any case; can award any punishment provided in BPC; can give death sentence but it must be approved by High Court [5, 6].  
   * *Additional District and Session Judge Court:* Can deal any case; can award any punishment provided in BPC; can give death sentence but it must be approved by High Court [5, 6].  
   * *Assistant District Session and Judge Court:* Can deal any civil case; can’t give death sentence; can give imprisonment up to 10 yrs [6].  
3. **Magistrate Court:**  
   * *First Class Magistrate Court:* Can deal criminal cases only; can give imprisonment up to 5 years including solitary confinement and fine up to 10 thousand taka; can't give death sentence [6].  
   * *Second Class Magistrate Court:* Can deal criminal cases only; can give imprisonment up to 3 years and fine up to 5 thousand taka; can't give death sentence [6, 7].  
   * *Third Class Magistrate Court:* Can deal criminal cases only; can give imprisonment up to 2 years and fine up to 2 thousand taka; can't give death sentence [7].  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-1, Page 22 & 23* [5-7]

### Q: When is a leading question allowed during Examination-in-Chief in court? Give a real-world example of using leading questions for "Identification Facts".
*(Credit: Afrin Tabassum)*

**Answer:**  
**Leading Question:** A question that suggests the answer which the examining person wishes to receive.  
Leading questions are generally not permitted during Examination-in-Chief except with the permission of the court:  
1. On introductory or undisputed matters.  
2. Regarding matters that have already been sufficiently proved.  
3. For the purpose of **identification** of a person or object (e.g., pointing towards a weapon and asking: *"Is this the weapon you examined?"* or pointing towards the accused and asking: *"Is this the person you examined for injuries?"*).  
4. When a witness turns hostile (with permission of the court).

## Chapter 5: Death with Apparent Death (Thanatology)

### Q: Medical classifications of death: Distinguish between cortical death, brainstem death, and whole-brain death. Why do clinicians differentiate them?
*(Credit: Mahia Rahman)*

**Answer:**  
**1. Cortical/Cerebral Death:** Permanent cessation of function of the cerebral cortex with an intact brain stem [16]. It produces a persistent vegetative state (PVS) where respiration and circulation continue spontaneously, but there is total loss of power of perception and cognition [16, 17].  
**2. Brainstem Death:** Cerebrum may be intact but is functionally cut off by a stem lesion [16]. There is permanent damage to the vital centers of respiration and the ascending reticular activating system (RAS), causing the victim to be irreversibly comatose and incapable of spontaneous breathing [16, 17].  
**3. Whole-Brain Death:** Permanent, irreversible cessation of function of the entire brain, including the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brain stem [16].  

**Why clinicians differentiate them:**  
* **Declaration and certification of death:** Brainstem death is legally recognized as somatic death, permitting a death certificate to be issued [18].  
* **Organ transplantation:** Living cadaver organs (heart, liver, kidneys, lungs) can only be retrieved while circulation is artificially maintained before whole-body biological collapse [6, 18, 19].  
* **Withdrawal of life-support:** To legally and ethically decide when to discontinue mechanical ventilation [18].  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-1, Page 142 & 145*

### Q: Why might rigor mortis fail to commence or appear absent in a dead body?
*(Credit: Oyshe)*

**Answer:**  
Rigor mortis may fail to be observed or appear absent due to:  
1. **Primary flaccidity stage:** The cadaver is examined too early (within 1–2 hours after death) before rigor mortis sets in [9, 32].  
2. **Cold/Freezing temperatures:** Extreme cold or freezing suspends chemical processes, postponing the onset of rigor mortis.  
3. **Advanced putrefaction:** Rigor mortis has already broken down and passed off due to autolysis and liquefaction of muscle proteins (secondary flaccidity) [35, 36].  
4. **Wasted muscles / Cachexia:** Severe emaciation, chronic wasting diseases, or severe malnutrition, where muscle glycogen and ATP reserves are negligible.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-1, Page 166 & 167*

### Q: Putrefaction: Mention the stages and anatomical location where the first color change appears.
*(Credit: Ashraful)*

**Answer:**  
* **First color change:** A greenish discoloration appears over the **right iliac fossa** (overlying the caecum, where the gut is closest to the anterior abdominal wall and rich in bacteria and $\text{H}_2\text{S}$) in about 12–24 hours in summer (24–36 hours in winter).  
* **Stages/Sequelae of Putrefaction:**  
  1. *Stage of color changes:* Greenish discoloration of right iliac fossa spreading to entire abdomen, marbling of skin veins.  
  2. *Stage of gas development:* Accumulation of putrefactive gases leading to bloating, protrusion of tongue and eyes, postmortem purge.  
  3. *Stage of colliquative putrefaction:* Liquefaction and melting down of tissues (5–12 days) [36].  
  4. *Stage of skeletonization:* Soft tissues dissolve away, leaving bones, cartilage, and ligaments (1–3 months) [36].

### Q: Environmental conditions where putrefaction, mummification, and adipocere formation can be observed simultaneously in the same cadaver.
*(Credit: Rubaiat Rahman)*

**Answer:**  
These changes can occur simultaneously in a dead body exposed to **irregular, microclimatic environmental conditions** or uneven burial:  
* A body lying partially immersed in stagnant water or wet soil while another portion is exposed to a warm, dry draft of air.  
* For example, the dependent lower body and fatty buttocks immersed in wet marsh develop **adipocere**, the exposed limbs in hot dry moving air undergo **mummification**, and the unventilated abdominal viscera undergo bacterial **putrefaction** [35].

### Q: What are the 6 standard post-mortem skin incisions used in forensic autopsy?
*(Credit: Hafiza Arjuman Sayma)*

**Answer:**  
The standard post-mortem incisions are:  
1. **I-shaped incision:** Midline incision extending from suprasternal notch (or symphysis menti) down to pubic symphysis, passing left to umbilicus.  
2. **Y-shaped incision:** Begins at acromion processes bilaterally, runs below breasts to xiphoid process, and continues down the midline to pubic symphysis.

### Q: Poisoning Autopsy Opinions: Why do doctors issue two opinions? Exact wording of Primary vs. Final opinion, and grammar/tense rules.
*(Credit: Sumaiya, Hafiza Arjuman Sayma, Md. Habibur Rahman, Oyshe, Nuha, Khondoker Rafid)*

**Answer:**  
* **Why two opinions:** Because autopsy findings in poisoning are often non-specific or inconclusive. The viscera and body fluids must be preserved and sent to the Forensic Chemical Examiner. Therefore, a provisional primary opinion is recorded, awaiting the chemical report.  
* **Primary Opinion:** Issued immediately after completing autopsy:  
  * *"Opinion as to the cause of death is withheld pending receipt of the chemical examiner's report of the preserved viscera."*  
* **Final Opinion:** Formulated in the **past tense** after correlating autopsy findings with the chemical examiner’s report [43]:  
  * *"Considering the postmortem examination findings and the chemical analysis report of the preserved viscera, I am of the opinion that the deceased died of violent asphyxia / toxic effects resulting from [Name of Poison], which was antemortem in nature."* [43]  
* **If chemical analysis is negative:** The doctor issues a reasoned opinion based on pathognomonic external/internal autopsy findings and exclusion of natural disease:  
  * *"Considering the negative chemical report but characteristic pathological autopsy findings and clinical history, death was due to..."* or records a **Negative Autopsy** if no lethal lesion exists [45].

### Q: Why do incised wounds bleed more profusely than lacerations?
*(Credit: Ashraful)*

**Answer:**  
In incised wounds, the blood vessels are **clean-cut** across by the sharp cutting edge, preventing retraction, crushing, and twisting of the vessel coats, so bleeding is free and profuse [14-16]. In lacerated wounds, vessels are crushed and torn with irregular retraction and vasospasm, leading to less hemorrhage [6, 14].

## Chapter 10: Forensic Ballistics

### Q: What is an abrasion collar and how does it form?

### Q: Differences between burns and scalds: Which lesion is clinically more painful?
*(Credit: Aurisha)*

**Answer:**  
* **Differences:** Burns are caused by dry heat (flame, hot solids, radiant heat) exceeding $44^\circ\text{C}$, causing singeing of hairs, blackening, charring, and dry brown/black eschars [57-59]. Scalds are caused by moist heat (hot liquids, steam) at or near boiling point; hair is never singed, skin is bleached, sodden, and water-logged without charring [59].

### Q: Fluid resuscitation calculations in burns:
1. *40 kg patient with 18% burns.*  
2. *50 kg patient with 10% burns.*  
*(Credit: Afroza Akter, Ashraful, Afrin Tabassum)*

**Answer:**  
Using the standard **Parkland Formula** for fluid resuscitation in the first 24 hours:  
$$\text{Total Fluid (Ringer's Lactate)} = 4\,\text{mL} \times \text{Weight (kg)} \times \text{\% Burn Surface Area}$$  

1. **For 40 kg patient with 18% burns:**  
   $$\text{Total Fluid} = 4\,\text{mL} \times 40 \times 18 = 2880\,\text{mL}$$  
   * *First 8 hours:* Half of the volume = **1440 mL**.  
   * *Next 16 hours:* Remaining half = **1440 mL**.  
2. **For 50 kg patient with 10% burns:**  
   $$\text{Total Fluid} = 4\,\text{mL} \times 50 \times 10 = 2000\,\text{mL}$$  
   * *First 8 hours:* Half of the volume = **1000 mL**.  
   * *Next 16 hours:* Remaining half = **1000 mL**.

### Q: Differences between Alternating Current (AC) and Direct Current (DC).
*(Credit: Khondoker Rafid)*

**Answer:**  
* **Alternating Current (AC):** Changes direction cyclically (typically 50–60 Hz in domestic supplies) [64]. AC is **4 to 5 times more dangerous** than DC at the same voltage because cyclic reversals stimulate continuous tetanic muscular spasms ("freeze-to-circuit" effect), preventing the victim from letting go, and predisposes the heart to fatal ventricular fibrillation [24, 64].  
* **Direct Current (DC):** Flows continuously in one direction (e.g., batteries, industrial dynamos). It tends to produce a single violent muscular contraction that throws the victim away from the conductor, often resulting in mechanical blast or blunt secondary fall injuries [64].  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 349 & 350*

### Q: What is an electric burn (Joule burn)? Describe its characteristics and factors influencing shock severity.
*(Credit: M, Khondoker Rafid)*

**Answer:**

* **Morphological Characteristics:**

### Q: What is Blast Lung? Pathophysiology, histological signs, and clinical problems.
*(Credit: Khondoker Rafid)*

**Answer:**

### Q: Blood Identification: Why is the Precipitin test definitive? Principles and mechanism.
*(Credit: Rashid)*

**Answer:**

## Chapter 26 to 38: Toxicology

### Q: What is the definition of poison? Local vs. systemic toxic actions.
*(Credit: Mahia Rahman)*

**Answer:**  
* **Definition of Poison:** A poison is a substance (solid, liquid, or gas) which, if introduced into the living body or brought into contact with any part thereof, will produce ill-health, disease, or death by its constitutional or local effects.  
* **Local Actions:** Direct destructive or inflammatory action at the site of contact (e.g., corrosion, blistering, irritation of GIT mucosa or skin) [49].  
* **Systemic Actions:** Toxic effects produced on distant target organs (CNS, CVS, respiratory system, liver, kidneys) after absorption into the bloodstream [49, 50].

### Q: What are Somatic Death, Molecular Death, Cause of Death, Manner of Death, and Mode of Death?
*(Credit: Photo 8 — Sirat Ma'am)*

**Answer:**  
* **Somatic / Systemic Death:** Irreversible cessation of the vital triad (*Bishop’s Tripod of Life* — nervous system, circulatory system, and respiratory system).  
* **Molecular / Cellular Death:** Death of individual cells and tissues following somatic death, occurring 1 to 4 hours later when cells deplete ATP and cellular irritability stops.

* **Manner of Death:** The legal circumstance under which death occurred: **Natural, Accidental, Suicidal, Homicidal, or Undetermined**.  
* **Mode of Death:** The physiological mechanism by which life ceases: **Coma** (nervous system failure), **Syncope** (circulatory failure), or **Asphyxia** (respiratory failure).

* **Incisions:** I-incision, Y-incision, Modified Y-incision, T-incision, Coronal scalp incision.  
* **Which is best and why:** The **I-incision** is the standard, best, and most universally practiced incision because it provides rapid, wide, and complete exposure of the neck organs, thorax, and abdominal cavity through a single continuous line.  
* **Why avoid the umbilicus:** The midline vertical incision cuts straight down to about 2 cm above the umbilicus, skirts around the **left side of the umbilicus**, and resumes in the midline down to the pubic symphysis. The umbilicus is avoided because:  
  1. It is a dense, fibrous, cicatricial remnant that is difficult to cut and suture neatly.  
  2. The round ligament of the liver (*ligamentum teres hepatis*, obliterated umbilical vein) attaches to the right of the umbilicus; skirting to the left prevents accidental injury to abdominal wall vessels and underlying falciform attachments.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 193 & 194*

### Q: How is the coronal scalp incision made? How far is the scalp reflected forward and backward?
*(Credit: Photo 6, 9, 10, 12, 13 — Saify Sir)*

**Answer:**  
* **Procedure:** Part the hair over the vertex. Using a scalpel, make a coronal incision starting behind one ear at the **mastoid process**, carried vertically across the **vertex** of the skull to the opposite **mastoid process**.  
* **Reflecting the flaps:**  
  * The **anterior scalp flap** is peeled and reflected forward down to **1 to 2 cm above the supraorbital ridges (eyebrows)**, exposing the frontal bone while protecting the orbital margins.  
  * The **posterior scalp flap** is reflected backward down to the **external occipital protuberance**, exposing the parietal and occipital bones.  
* **Skull opening:** A circular saw cut is placed horizontally around the circumference of the calvarium using a hand saw or oscillating autopsy saw, followed by gentle separation using a skull chisel and hammer.

### Q: What is an Obscure Autopsy? How does it differ from a Negative Autopsy? Mention its causes.
*(Credit: Photo 10, 11, 13 — Saify Sir)*

**Answer:**  
* **Negative Autopsy:** An autopsy where meticulous gross and microscopic dissection, toxicological analysis, and laboratory investigations **fail to reveal any structural, anatomical, or toxicological cause of death**.  
* **Obscure Autopsy:** An autopsy where the pathological or toxicological findings are **vague, minimal, unconvincing, or difficult to interpret**, leaving the examiner unable to definitively state whether the lesion caused death or was incidental.  
* **Causes of Obscure Autopsy:**  
  1. Reflex vagal inhibition (death from shock after blunt blow to sensitive areas).  
  2. Subtle electrolyte imbalance (e.g., hyperkalemia, hypokalemia).  
  3. Undetectable plant or synthetic poisons, or delayed death where poison has been completely eliminated.  
  4. Concealed trauma (blunt impact to cervical spine, subtle concussion).  
  5. Early stages of myocardial ischemia before microscopic infarction changes develop.  
  6. Air or fat embolism without visible gross obstruction.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 198 & 205* [16, 19, 23]

### Q: Viscera collection: What viscera are preserved? Where are they sent, who takes them, and what preservatives are used?

* **Viscera collected routinely:**

### Q: Define Artefacts. Classify them, give examples of resuscitative artefacts, and explain their misinterpretation.
*(Credit: Photo 8, 9, 10, 11, 13 — Saify Sir)*

**Answer:**  
* **Definition of Artefact:** An artefact is any artificial change, structure, feature, or finding introduced into the body during life, at or after death, which did not exist naturally, and which may lead to incorrect interpretation of the cause, manner, or time of death.  
* **Classification of Artefacts:**  
  1. *Agonal / Terminal artefacts:* Vomit aspiration during agony.  
  2. *Post-mortem artefacts:* Livor mortis, rigor mortis, putrefaction changes, animal bites (ants, rats, maggots).  
  3. *Resuscitative artefacts:* Introduced during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).  
  4. *Autopsy / Morgue artefacts:* Incisions, saw cuts, organ tearing during extraction.  
  5. *Toxicological artefacts:* Contaminants in viscera bottles or preservative errors.  
* **Examples of Resuscitative Artefacts:**  
  * Fracture of ribs and sternum during external chest compressions.  
  * Cardiac and pulmonary contusions from vigorous cardiac massage.  
  * Needle puncture marks over the heart, cubital veins, or neck from intracardiac/intravenous emergency drug injections.  
  * Circular or rectangular superficial burns/erythema on chest skin from defibrillator pads.  
  * Mucosal tears of pharynx, larynx, or vocal cords from difficult endotracheal intubation.  
* **Misinterpretation:** Resuscitative rib and sternal fractures can be **misinterpreted as homicidal blunt force assault or vehicular trauma**, and defibrillator electrical burns or injection marks can be **misinterpreted as physical torture or deliberate violence**.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-4, Page 515 & 516* [25]

## Chapter 11: Violent Asphyxial Deaths

### Q: Define Suffocation. What are the cardinal signs of Asphyxia?
*(Credit: Photo 8 — Sirat Ma'am)*

**Answer:**  
* **Definition of Suffocation:** Asphyxial death caused by mechanical hindrance to the entry of atmospheric air into the respiratory tract by means other than constriction of the neck (includes smothering, choking, gagging, traumatic/crush asphyxia, and environmental suffocation).  
* **Cardinal Post-Mortem Signs of Asphyxia:**  
  1. Intense cyanosis of face, lips, and nail beds.  
  2. Dark, purplish, abnormally fluid blood (due to high fibrinolysin activity).  
  3. Petechial hemorrhages (**Tardieu spots**) under the pleura, pericardium, and conjunctiva.  
  4. Generalized visceral and pulmonary venous congestion.  
  5. Engorgement of the right side of the heart while the left ventricle is empty.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 288 & 291*

---

### Q: Hanging vs. Strangulation: In which one are external asphyxial signs more prominent and why?
*(Credit: Photo 8 — Sirat Ma'am)*

**Answer:**  
Asphyxial signs (cyanosis, facial congestion, petechial hemorrhages) are **much more marked and prominent in Strangulation**.  
**Reason:** In hanging, the weight of the suspended body exerts sudden, high tension on the ligature, occluding both jugular veins and carotid arteries simultaneously, or triggering rapid reflex vagal cardiac arrest, producing a pale face with minimal petechiae. In strangulation, venous occlusion is gradual and sustained while arterial flow continues, resulting in intense venous backpressure, cyanosis, and profuse petechial hemorrhages in the face and eyes.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 298 & 309*

---

### Q: When can the ligature mark be absent in hanging?
*(Credit: Photo 13 — Sathi Ma'am)*

**Answer:**  
The ligature mark may be faint, indistinct, or completely absent:  
1. When a **soft, broad, padded ligature material** is used (e.g., silk saree, soft woollen scarf, cotton orna, or folded towel).  
2. When the body is **cut down immediately** after suspension before post-mortem drying (parchmentization) sets in.  
3. When clothing or a thick beard intervenes between the neck skin and the ligature rope.  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 298 & 299*

### Q: Fresh Water vs. Salt Water Drowning: State the pathophysiology / cause of death in one line.
*(Credit: Photo 8 — Sirat Ma'am)*

**Answer:**  
* **Fresh water drowning:** Causes massive water absorption across alveoli into circulation leading to **hemodilution, hypervolemia, hemolysis, hyperkalemia, and fatal ventricular fibrillation** (death in 2–5 minutes).  
* **Salt water (sea) drowning:** Causes hypertonic fluid drawing plasma out of capillaries into alveoli leading to **hemoconcentration, hypovolemia, massive pulmonary edema, and acute anoxic asphyxia** (death in 5–8 minutes).  

**Page Reference:** *Concept FM P-2, Page 317*
